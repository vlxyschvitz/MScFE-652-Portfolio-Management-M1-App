Lesson 2

Value at Risk

Variance treats gains and losses symmetrically. A risk manager usually wants one number that answers a sharper question: how much could I lose, over a given horizon, with a given confidence? That number is Value at Risk.

Definition

At confidence level \alpha (e.g. 95%), the VaR of a portfolio is the loss threshold exceeded with probability 1-\alpha:

Three ways to estimate it

  • Historical simulation — sort the observed returns and read off the empirical (1-\alpha) percentile. No distributional assumption, but limited by the sample you have.
  • Parametric (variance–covariance) — assume returns are normal and use the sample mean and std directly:

  • Monte Carlo — simulate thousands of paths from a fitted model and take the empirical percentile of the simulated outcomes. Slower, but flexible enough for non-normal or path-dependent positions.

Lesson 3

From Utility Theory to Classical Portfolio Theory

Why should mean and variance be the only things an investor cares about? The answer comes from expected utility theory — and it's the hinge on which the rest of the module turns.

Expected utility & risk aversion

A rational investor is assumed to maximise expected utility of terminal wealth E[U(W)], not expected wealth itself. A concave U means the investor is risk averse: losses hurt more than equal-sized gains help.

The bridge to mean–variance

Quadratic utility is special: it makes expected utility a function of only the mean and variance of wealth, with no other moments involved. The Mathematics of Classical Portfolio Theory

With mean–variance preferences justified, Markowitz's problem becomes pure linear algebra: given \hat{\boldsymbol\mu} and \hat{\boldsymbol\Sigma}, find the weights that minimise variance for a given return — and trace out the set of such portfolios.

Adding a risk-free asset

Once a risk-free rate r_f is available, every rational investor holds a mix of that asset and a single risky portfolio: the tangency portfolio that maximises the Sharpe ratio.

The set of portfolios combining r_f and the tangency portfolio forms the Capital Market Line:

That line dominates every point on the risky-only frontier except where it touches — which is exactly the outcome Lesson 3's indifference curves are drawn toward: each investor just picks the point on the CML that matches their own \lambda.

Synthesis

The loop closes

The four lessons aren't four separate topics — they're one pipeline, run in a circle:

1 → 2

Lesson 1's sample mean and covariance matrix are exactly the inputs the parametric VaR formula needs. Fatter tails (Lesson 1's kurtosis) are precisely where historical and parametric VaR start to disagree.

2 → 3

A VaR number is silent on how much an investor should be willing to pay to avoid it. Utility theory supplies that price, via risk aversion \lambda and the certainty equivalent.

3 → 4

Quadratic utility (or joint normality) collapses preferences onto the mean–variance plane, which is exactly the objective Markowitz optimisation minimises.

4 → 1

The optimal portfolio's weights feed straight back into Lesson 1's formulas for \hat\mu_p and \hat\sigma_p^2 — whose risk you would then, once again, want to bound with a VaR figure.
